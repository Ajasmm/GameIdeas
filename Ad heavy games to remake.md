# Sort puzzles beat everything for 42-hour remakes

The best target is the **sort-puzzle family (water sort, ball sort, flat 2D screw sort)**. These games have 100M-class audiences and the same recurring 1-star complaints: an ad after every level, ad time longer than play time, and "No Ads" purchases that still show ads. Their art is just coloured shapes, and levels can be generated procedurally, so a solo Unity developer with AI coding help can realistically build one in ~42 hours. Next come a **"fair" block puzzle** in the Block Blast mould (the biggest audience, but also the hardest incumbent to beat), a **top-down 2D parking/unblock puzzle**, and a **pin-pull puzzle**. On PC, the opening is different: F2P idle and clicker games (DPS Idle 2, Summoners War: RUSH, Farmer Against Potatoes Idle) get Mixed reviews for paywalls and mobile-style ads. **Paid, fair idlers** such as Rusty's Retirement and Gnorp Apologue show that players will pay for the fair version. Two caveats matter more than the build. First, "fewer ads" alone is a weak moat against incumbents with hundreds of millions of installs, so discovery is the real bottleneck. Second, a one-week build is not a one-week launch: Google Play's closed-testing gate for new personal accounts and the ads/IAP integration work stretch the calendar well past seven days. Install counts for several titles below come from aggregators or are unverified, as flagged.

## What to fix: honour the purchase, end the per-level ad tax

The complaints repeat across titles closely enough to serve as a spec. In four separate games (Water Sort, Ball Sort, Screw Puzzle, Parking Jam 3D), **players paid to remove ads and still saw them**. The cheapest and most marketable fix is a one-time "remove forced ads" purchase that is fully honoured, with optional rewarded ads still available. Next on the list: no ads mid-level, freezing or black screens around ads, undo and extra-tube tools that aren't gated behind ads, and progress that never gets lost. Players praise the same core loop in every review: relaxing, one-finger, no timer, offline, thousands of levels, clean visuals ([Marlvel](https://marlvel.ai/intel-report/games/ball-sort-puzzle); [GameBrain](https://gamebrain.co/game/screw-puzzle-nuts-and-bolts)). Small install size also helped Water Sort grow ([SocialPeta](https://socialpeta.com/blog/water%20sort-color-puzzle-game-surpasses-100-million-downloads)). So the remake should improve on fairness and stability, not graphics.

The competition is not empty. Sort It Out and Zen Sort are reportedly ad-free already, and most leaders sell ad removal for $3–5. That claim comes from an ad-blocker vendor ([AdLock](https://adlock.com/blog/sorting-games-without-ads)). Each remake therefore needs a twist of its own on top of fair ads, such as a theme, a new mechanic, or daily challenges. This also matters for legal reasons, covered below.

## The 42-hour budget is mostly not gameplay

With AI writing most of the code, the core loop and input take only **6–8 hours**. The rest of a realistic budget, all estimates, looks like this. Levels (a generator plus a solvability check, or 30–60 hand-tuned levels) take 8–10 h and are the biggest risk. Juice (tweens, sound, haptics) takes 4–6 h. UI, saves and settings take 4 h. The ads SDK with mediation, UMP consent and Play Billing takes 5–7 h, and Unity's Gradle conflicts often eat into that. Store assets, privacy policy and Data Safety form take 4 h. Low-end device testing takes 4–6 h. This is why sort and block puzzles rank first: their level generation is algorithmic and needs almost no art. The developer should build a reusable kit in game one (ads wrapper, IAP, save, level select). That saves about 10 hours on each later game. Industry practice backs fast iteration (Voodoo tests about 2,000 prototypes a year), but those are prototypes, not shipped products ([Deconstructor of Fun](https://www.deconstructoroffun.com/blog/2025/06/02/how-voodoo-builds-hits-four-product-lessons-from-a-2000-prototypes-a-year-machine)). On PC, Rusty's Retirement started as a **two-week solo prototype** and sold about 330k copies at $7 ([GameDiscoverCo](https://newsletter.gamediscover.co/p/how-rustys-retirement-idle-farmed)). Gnorp took about 3.5 years ([GameDiscoverCo](https://newsletter.gamediscover.co/p/how-this-solo-dev-incremental-game)). A 42-hour PC build is a starting point for a paid release, not the finished product. Avoid survivors-likes: in 2026 that genre is saturated, and players easily spot reskins ([How To Market A Game](https://howtomarketagame.com/2026/05/19/2026-state-of-bullet-heavens-how-vital-shell-succeeded/)).

All of these candidates work with simple 2D sketch-style art. Lock one style prompt and a reference image, use a 5–6 colour palette, and build the UI in-engine. Purely AI-generated images cannot be copyrighted in the US after the Supreme Court declined Thaler in March 2026 ([PetaPixel](https://petapixel.com/2026/03/04/supreme-court-declines-to-hear-ai-image-copyright-case/)). An artist's later refinement adds authorship that can be protected. Steam's January 2026 form requires disclosing pre-generated AI content that players consume, but not AI coding tools ([PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/software/ai/steam-updates-ai-disclosure-form-to-specify-that-its-focused-on-ai-generated-content-that-is-consumed-by-players-not-efficiency-tools-used-behind-the-scenes/)). Galaxy Idle Clicker's recent reviews turned mostly negative after it reportedly switched to AI art ([Notebookcheck](https://www.notebookcheck.net/Steam-launch-New-free-to-play-idle-clicker-game-hits-Steam-faces-immediate-backlash-over-artwork.1081692.0.html)). On Steam, artist refinement is commercially important, not optional.

## Legal and policy lines: copy the rules, never the expression

Game rules and genre ideas are free to use. Expression is not. In Tetris v. Xio a court found a Tetris clone infringing ([PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/court-declares-tetris-clone-a-breach-of-copyright-big-implications-for-derivative-games/)). In Spry Fox v. Lolapps, the object hierarchy and even names counted as protectable, despite different art ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spry_Fox,_LLC_v._Lolapps,_Inc.)). Google Play bans apps that "merely provide the same experience as other apps" without adding value, and it bans multiple highly similar apps from one developer ([Google Play Spam policy](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9899034?hl=en)). It also bans names and icons confusingly similar to existing products, and keyword stuffing ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/google-play-puts-foot-down-on-storefront-mimicry)). In practice that means: your own name, icon, palette, theme and levels; at least one new mechanic; and never the competitor's name in the listing or keywords. Generic genres like sort or parking carry less risk than games tied to IP owners who enforce aggressively, such as Tetris. No game-specific Indian precedent was found, and none of this is legal advice.

The ad rules set hard limits that line up with the "fair" pitch. Google bans interstitials that appear unexpectedly mid-activity or at the start of a level. It allows them at natural breaks, such as after a score screen, and allows opt-in rewarded ads ([Better Ads policy](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/12271244?hl=en)). Interstitials that can't be closed after 15 seconds are banned, and so are ads that imitate the app's own UI ([Ads policy](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9857753?hl=en)). A "No ads" claim in the listing must stay literally true. "No forced ads" is the safer wording.

## Monetization and launch: fair ads earn less per player, so retention must pay

Fewer interstitials do trade revenue for retention. ZiMAD found that adding Day-1 interstitials raised ARPDAU 18% and cut D7 retention by 2.5% ([Unity](https://unity.com/en/resources/zimad-dig-out)). Voodoo often holds interstitials back early in a game ([Deconstructor of Fun](https://www.deconstructoroffun.com/blog/2025/06/02/how-voodoo-builds-hits-four-product-lessons-from-a-2000-prototypes-a-year-machine)). The suggested model is an estimate to A/B test. Make rewarded ads the main format (hints, undo, continues, doublers). Show no interstitials for the first ~10 levels or 1–3 sessions. After that, show them only at level end and cap them at about one every 2–3 minutes. Run no gameplay banners. Sell a one-time "remove forced ads" IAP. Ad rates differ sharply by region. Android rewarded eCPMs run about $30 in the US and about $8 in APAC, and India rewarded eCPMs fell 5% from Q1 to Q2 2026 ([Playio](https://blog.playio.co/mobile-game-ecpm-benchmarks-2026)). Store listings should therefore target Tier-1 English markets, not depend on Indian installs. On PC the model is simpler: a $3–7 premium price with no ads, plus an optional supporter DLC, which is Rusty's approach.

Launch realities decide the outcome more than the build does. New Google Play personal developer accounts must run a closed test with **12 testers for 14 days** before production access. This comes from background knowledge, not a verified source, so confirm it in the current Play Console rules. Plan for it by starting closed testing of game one in the build week and recruiting testers early. Paid user acquisition rarely pays back on ad-monetized games at small budgets. The realistic route is organic ASO with a portfolio of games. Mine the target game's 1–2 star reviews, put the top two fixes in the icon and first screenshot, and use terms like "offline," "no wifi" and "relaxing," which apps already rank on ([AppGoblin](https://appgoblin.info/apps/6737501846/keywords)). Space out releases so they don't trigger Play's similar-apps spam rule. On Steam, Gnorp shows growth can come from YouTube even with only ~1.7k launch wishlists ([GameDiscoverCo](https://newsletter.gamediscover.co/p/how-this-solo-dev-incremental-game)). But Steam's wishlist cycle means PC titles need months of lead time.

## Conclusion

The opportunity is not a game nobody has made. It is a broken promise that millions of players have already paid for and been let down by: "remove ads" that doesn't remove ads, and play time eaten by ad time. That makes the honoured purchase, not the mechanic, the product. A sort puzzle is the right first project because it costs the least to build, so most of the 42 hours go into the reusable ads/IAP/save kit that every later game shares. The order is: one 2D sort game, with closed testing started immediately; then a block or parking puzzle reusing the kit; then a premium PC idler, once mobile data shows whether fair ads really retain better.

The main unknowns are verified Play install counts for most candidates, a measured link between fewer forced ads and higher ratings, and whether the "fair" revenue trade-off pays off at small scale. Treat the first release as an experiment that answers those questions, not as the business.
